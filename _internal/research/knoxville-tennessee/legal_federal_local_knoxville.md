# Purebaric in Knoxville: federal and local rules for renting soft chambers with oxygen concentrators

Prepared 2026-10-10 for Purebaric (Utah LLC). Scope: **federal** rules plus **local** rules for the City of Knoxville, Knox County, Maryville, Alcoa, Oak Ridge and Farragut. Tennessee state statutes, boards, taxes and waiver law are covered in a separate memo.

> **This is research, not legal advice.** It was put together from public sources read on the dates shown. Wherever a source could not be read directly, the item is marked **NOT VERIFIED**. Have a Tennessee-licensed attorney, and the Knoxville Fire Marshal for the fire-code questions, confirm everything here before you rely on it.

---

## (a) Executive summary (10 lines)

1. The chambers we found cleared by FDA, one soft chamber (K072757, 1.2 to 1.3 ATA) and one semi-rigid chamber (K220290, up to 1.5 ATA, indicated for use at physician offices and health institutions), are **Class II, prescription-only devices** cleared only to treat **acute mountain sickness**. Both are inflated with ordinary air. **None of the reviewed clearances covers oxygen use or wellness use.** Purebaric's own chamber make, model and 510(k) number are not yet recorded here and must be added.
2. FDA's prescription-device rule (21 CFR 801.109) allows retail distributors to hold Rx devices, but those devices may be supplied **only to, or on the order of, a licensed practitioner**. Renting them with no prescription takes away the labelling exemption, and the device is then treated as misbranded.
3. The oxygen concentrators are also Class II and cleared for **prescription use**. Using them to deliver 95 to 99% oxygen **inside** a chamber cleared for air only goes beyond any clearance we found.
4. FDA's general-wellness policy (revised 2026-01-06) covers only **low-risk** products. FDA also looks at whether it "actively regulates" the same product type, and it does regulate hyperbaric chambers. The "not a medical device" disclaimer therefore probably does not take the product out of FDA's reach. Under 21 CFR 801.4, a seller who promotes a new intended use has to supply labelling for that use.
5. **The biggest local issue is the fire code.** Knoxville adopted the **2024 International Fire Code** (Code §11-21). IFC §609 requires hyperbaric facilities to follow **NFPA 99**, and IBC §425 requires NFPA 99 **Chapter 14** for those facilities. Knoxville has no local amendment that exempts mild chambers.
6. Other fire officials already treat mild chambers as hyperbaric facilities. Minnesota's State Fire Marshal applies §609 and NFPA 99 Ch. 14 to them. Raleigh, Charlotte and Durham ordered mild-chamber wellness services to stop in 2022. An NFPA proposal to create a lighter category for low-pressure chambers failed **0 to 17** in 2023. Expect the Knoxville Fire Marshal could do the same at a gym or studio.
7. FTC: health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence", usually randomized controlled trials, and a disclaimer does not fix a deceptive claim. The FTC has warned hyperbaric sellers before (2020 and 2022, over COVID claims). Keep claims to relaxation and comfort only.
8. Business licences: today, as an out-of-state seller with no Tennessee location, Purebaric registers for state business tax through TNTAP. **Once it has a local base (warehouse or home stock), it needs a Knox County licence and a City of Knoxville licence ($15 each)**, and similar city and county licences elsewhere.
9. Zoning: a Knoxville home base is possible under the home-occupation rules if all stock is kept indoors and vans do not gather there for dispatch. Unincorporated Knox County is stricter (household members only, no accessory buildings). A warehouse is permitted in the I-MU, I-RD, I-G and I-H districts.
10. Sales tax: the local rate is 2.25% in Knox County, Knoxville and Farragut, and 2.75% in Blount (Maryville, Alcoa) and Anderson (Oak Ridge). The health department, DOT hazmat rules and local news raised no issues.

## (c) Detailed findings

### 1. FDA status of soft mild hyperbaric chambers

**Classification.** 21 CFR 868.5470 defines a hyperbaric chamber as a device "intended to increase the environmental oxygen pressure to promote the movement of oxygen from the environment to a patient's tissue by means of pressurization that is greater than atmospheric pressure". It is **Class II**, product code **CBF** [1].

**K072757: Summit to Sea "Shallow Dive", "Dive" and "Grand Dive".** Cleared 2008-02-08; the corrected SE letter was dated on receipt of the 2008-01-28 submission [2].
- The summary describes "mild hyperbaric chambers for pressures less than 5 PSI" (1.2 to 1.3 ATA), with a bag "inflated with atmospheric air with a small electric pump".
- Indications for use: "Acute Mountain sickness (AMS) as prescribed by or under the direction of a physician".
- It carries the caution "Federal Law restricts this device to sale by or on the order of a physician", and the **Prescription Use** box is checked.
- Predicates: the Gamow Bag (K874752A) and HTI Model-2 (K001409).
- Nothing in the summary authorises oxygen.

**K220290: Revitalair 430+ (US Hyperbaric Network).** Cleared 2023-05-04 [3].
- It is a semi-rigid chamber operating at "no greater than 1.5 atmospheres absolute", "inflated with atmospheric air through an electric compressor".
- Indication: "to treat acute mountain sickness under the prescription of a health professional", "designed for use at physician offices and health institutions".
- The comparison table lists "Prescription – Rx Only: Yes". Predicate: Revitalair 430F (K171899).

**"Do not use with oxygen".** Neither summary contains that exact phrase (verified by reading both PDFs). Both describe air-only inflation.
- The UHMS position statement (revised 2018-07-10) says these chambers "are designed to be compressed only with air" and that "the FDA prohibits the use of these devices with supplemental oxygen" [17]. That is UHMS's description; **the FDA document behind it was NOT VERIFIED**.
- A UHMS Q1 2025 article says concentrators are "not authorized to be used in conjunction with fabric, soft-sided chambers" [18].
- A UHMS sidebar repeats a manufacturer's claim that one soft chamber (SOS "Hyperlite") has an oxygen clearance. **NOT VERIFIED.** Check the 510(k) database before relying on it.

**What Rx-only status requires of a renter (21 CFR 801.109).** A prescription device is exempt from the "adequate directions for use" requirement (FD&C Act §502(f)(1)) only if two conditions are met [4]:
- it is (a)(1)(i) "in the possession of a person ... regularly and lawfully engaged in the manufacture, transportation, storage, or wholesale or retail distribution of such device"; and
- it (a)(2) "is to be sold only to or on the prescription or other order of such practitioner".

Purebaric can lawfully hold the devices as a retail distributor. Supplying them to consumers with no practitioner's order fails condition (a)(2), so the device loses the exemption and is misbranded. Altering labelling or doing "any other act" that results in misbranding while the device is "held for sale (whether or not the first sale)" is a prohibited act under 21 U.S.C. 331(k) [44].
- **Does renting count as "sale" or "distribution"?** Under 21 CFR 807.3(b), "commercial distribution" means distribution of a device "held or offered for sale" [5]. No FDA document read for this memo says in so many words that renting is "sale", but FDA and the courts have generally read "held for sale" broadly. **NOT VERIFIED for rental specifically.** Assume renting is treated as distribution.

**Intended use and the disclaimer (21 CFR 801.4).** Intended use is judged by "objective intent", shown by labelling, advertising, "the design or composition of the article" and "the circumstances surrounding the distribution". If "a packer, distributor, or seller intends an article for different uses than those intended by the person from whom he or she received the article, such ... seller is required to supply adequate labeling in accordance with the new intended uses" [45].
- A pressurized chamber with an oxygen concentrator fits the §868.5470 definition by design, whatever the website disclaimer says.
- Purebaric's "general wellness" positioning is itself a new intended use that would need its own labelling and, in practice, its own clearance.

**FDA General Wellness guidance (2026-01-06, replacing the 2019 version).**
- It applies "only to general wellness products that are low risk".
- A product is not low risk if it "involve[s] an intervention or technology that may pose a risk to the safety of users ... if specific regulatory controls are not applied".
- FDA "recommends that you also consider whether CDRH actively regulates products of the same type". Its example: a device type regulated as Class II "would not be considered low risk general wellness products" [9].
- The guidance does not mention hyperbaric chambers. Applying it to them is our inference, but hyperbaric chambers are an actively regulated Class II type.

**Registration (21 CFR 807).** These categories are exempt from establishment registration [5]:
- 807.65(e): retail establishments "making final delivery or sale to the ultimate user";
- 807.65(i): persons "who dispense devices to the ultimate consumer or whose major responsibility is to render a service necessary to provide the consumer ... with a device".

A renter that does not relabel, repackage or import should fall within these. However:
- **importing chambers directly from a foreign maker** makes the company an "initial importer", which must register (807.3(d)(2), (g));
- **relabelling** (for example adding its own "wellness" labels to the device) counts as "manufacture" under 807.3(d)(1).

**Complaint records.** A "distributor" must keep device complaint files under 21 CFR 803.18(d) [6]. 21 CFR 803.3(e) defines a distributor as one who furthers marketing "to the person who makes final delivery or sale to the ultimate user". **Whether a renter making final delivery is itself a "distributor" under 803 is unclear (NOT VERIFIED).** Keeping complaint files costs little either way.

### 2. Oxygen concentrators and recent FDA communications

- **Classification.** Under 21 CFR 868.5440, a "portable oxygen generator" is a device "intended to release oxygen for respiratory therapy by means of ... physical means (e.g., a molecular sieve)". It is **Class II**, product code CAW [1].
- **Example clearance.** K250671 (CAIRE FreeStyle Comfort, 2025-07-11) is "used on a prescriptive basis by patients requiring supplemental oxygen", with "Rx Required: Yes" and the **Prescription Use** box checked [7].
  - No 10 L stationary unit's 510(k) was pulled for this memo. **Confirm the exact model's labelling.**
  - All electric concentrator clearances we saw are Rx-only. **NOT VERIFIED** that no over-the-counter concentrator exists.
- **FDA consumer pages have changed.** FDA's earlier consumer update said concentrators should be used only with a prescription and that FDA had not cleared any for over-the-counter use. That URL now serves "Pulse Oximeter Basics" (content current as of 2025-03-26), and the concentrator text is gone (checked 2026-10-10).
  - The 2021 consumer update "Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy: Get the Facts" and the 2013 "Don't Be Misled" page both returned **404 on fda.gov** on 2026-10-10. A cached copy (cacmap.fda.gov) appears in search results.
  - As quoted in search results, those pages warn that HBOT devices are not proven to treat cancer, Lyme disease, autism or Alzheimer's, and recommend UHMS-accredited facilities. **Current FDA text NOT VERIFIED.** Removing the pages does not change the regulations.
- **August 2025 FDA letter to health care providers** [8]. The letter, "Follow Instructions for Safe Use of HBOT Devices" (2025-08-25), followed the January 2025 Troy, Michigan death and the 2025 Arizona death.
  - It warns of "a heightened risk of fire with use of oxygen at a high concentration".
  - It calls for grounding, staff training, continuous supervision, manufacturer maintenance, keeping prohibited items out of the chamber, and cotton clothing. It cites NFPA 99-2024.
  - FDA-cleared HBOT devices "can be identified by searching for Product Code CBF".
  - It does not mention soft or mild chambers, concentrators, or home or wellness settings.
- **FDA letters to soft-chamber sellers 2025 to 2026.** None found in three searches of FDA and general sources (not exhaustive). The only hyperbaric warning letter found is to Ocean Hyperbaric Neurologic Center (about 2009, promotion for uncleared uses), via secondary source. **NOT VERIFIED on fda.gov.**

### 3. FTC claim substantiation

- **FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022)** [10]:
  - health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence", and randomized controlled trials are generally what experts require;
  - anecdotes, animal or in-vitro studies are not enough;
  - a disclaimer "won't cure an otherwise deceptive ad", and a disclosure "can't directly contradict a claim";
  - testimonials cannot carry claims the advertiser could not substantiate directly, and "results not typical" is not enough;
  - implied disease claims, for example "recovery" that suggests injury treatment, must also be substantiated.
- **FTC letters involving hyperbaric providers:**
  - McDonagh Medical Center (2020) and Gonino Center for Healing (2020): HBOT promoted for COVID-19;
  - Kimbertouch Technologies (2020): "hyperbaric style breathing";
  - **Ohana Hyperbarics (2022-02-22)**: cease-and-desist over social posts presenting HBOT as a COVID treatment, with written certification required within 48 hours [11][12].
  - No FTC hyperbaric action found for 2023 to 2026 (two searches, not exhaustive).
- **Safer language:** "relaxation", "a quiet place to rest", "a calm space in your recovery routine", "general wellness", "comfort". Do not cite pressure or oxygen benefit figures.
- **Unsafe language:** any disease or condition (autism, concussion or TBI, Lyme, cancer, long COVID, wounds, inflammation, "heals", "treats", "detox", "boosts immunity"), "HBOT", "oxygen therapy", "FDA-approved" or "FDA-cleared" (misleading for an off-label use, and 21 CFR 807.97 bars citing clearance as approval), "medical-grade", athletic-performance claims without RCTs, and "faster injury recovery".
- **Note:** this marketing advice is our reading of the FTC guidance applied to Purebaric, not an FTC statement.

### 4. Transport (DOT) and workplace (OSHA)

- **DOT hazmat.** A "hazardous material" is one designated under 49 U.S.C. 5103, including entries in the 49 CFR 172.101 table [13]. The table lists "Oxygen, compressed" (UN1072), refrigerated liquid oxygen (UN1073) and chemical oxygen generators (UN3356). **It has no entry for electric molecular-sieve concentrators**, which store no oxygen (table text searched 2026-10-10).
  - **Inference:** carrying a plain electric concentrator by van is not hazmat transport. This would change if Purebaric carried **oxygen cylinders** or concentrators with lithium batteries (portable units).
- **Vehicle.** A vehicle counts as an FMCSA "commercial motor vehicle" in interstate commerce only at **10,001 lb GVWR** or more, or when carrying placarded hazmat (49 CFR 390.5) [14]. A cargo van under that weight is outside the federal motor-carrier safety rules.
- **OSHA.**
  - 29 CFR 1910.104 covers only bulk oxygen systems over 13,000 cu ft [15].
  - 1910.146 (permit-required confined spaces) defines an "oxygen enriched atmosphere" as more than 23.5% O2 and a confined space as one an *employee* enters to perform work and that is "not designed for continuous employee occupancy" [16]. A gym client resting in a chamber is not an employee doing assigned work. Staff who zip clients in or adjust valves from outside are not entering it either.
  - No OSHA standard specific to mild chambers was found. The general-duty clause (OSH Act §5(a)(1)) would apply to recognised hazards such as fire with enriched oxygen and static. **No interpretation letter was searched (NOT VERIFIED).**

### 5. Business licensing: Knoxville, Knox County and adjacent cities

- **State rule (TN DOR Business Tax Manual, June 2025)** [19]:
  - Out-of-state businesses with nexus are subject to state-level business tax. Being "engaged in this state" includes "the leasing of tangible personal property that is located in this state".
  - "Out-of-state business entities are exempt from the municipal-level business tax". Without a Tennessee location, "business licenses are generally not required nor issued", and they register "directly with the Department through TNTAP".
  - Persons who "do not have a physical location, outlet, or other place of business within a municipality ... shall not be subject to the municipal-level business tax".
  - Once there is a location, a minimal-activity licence is needed "for each location at which its gross sales are more than $3,000 but less than $100,000", and a standard licence at $100,000 or more. The fee is **$15 to each county and, if applicable, city**.
  - An employee's home office alone is not a business location.
  - Contractors have separate rules that do not apply here.
- **Knox County Clerk, Business Tax Division** [20]: $15 to apply; minimal-activity licences renew yearly for $15; 300 Main St (Old Courthouse); 865-215-2392. "Businesses inside Knoxville city limits must also register with the City of Knoxville."
- **City of Knoxville Business License/Tax Office** [21]: City County Building, 400 Main St, Room 450; 865-215-2083; CityTaxOffice@KnoxvilleTN.gov. A business inside city limits needs **both** city and county licences.
- **Maryville** [22]: businesses with more than $3,000 of receipts need city and Blount County licences; $15 renewal; City Hall 865-273-3456, Blount County Clerk 865-273-5800.
- **Alcoa, Oak Ridge, Farragut:** no official licence pages read. Under the state framework, a $15 city licence applies only if the city levies municipal business tax **and** Purebaric has a location there. **NOT VERIFIED** which of these cities levy it.
- **Practical reading.** With no Tennessee premises, Purebaric registers with TNTAP only. If it keeps chambers in a Knoxville storage unit, home or warehouse, that is likely a "physical location, outlet, or other place of business": it then needs Knox County and Knoxville licences and pays municipal-level tax there. **Whether a self-storage unit or an employee's home with stock counts as a location is NOT VERIFIED.** Ask DOR.

### 6. Fire code

- **City of Knoxville** [23][24]:
  - Code §11-4 defines "Fire code" as "the 2024 International Fire Code, including appendices B, C, D and O". §11-21(a) adopts it (Ord. O-145-2024, 12-10-24).
  - §11-21(b): for codes and standards in IFC §102.7 and chapter 80, "the fire marshal will reference the latest published edition", which means the current NFPA 99.
  - §11-22 amends only fireworks (NFPA 1123) and the appeals board. **There is no local amendment on oxygen, medical gas or hyperbaric facilities.**
  - §11-1 permit fees cover only fireworks and fuel tanks.
  - Plans Review lists the 2024 IBC, 2024 IRC, 2024 IFC "with Local Amendments", and others.
- **IFC 2024 §609 "Hyperbaric facilities"** (identical in IFC 2018): "Hyperbaric facilities shall be inspected, tested and maintained in accordance with NFPA 99." Records of testing and repair "shall be available to the fire code official" [26][27].
- **IBC 2024 §425.1:** "Hyperbaric facilities shall meet the requirements contained in Chapter 14 of NFPA 99" [28]. This applies through building permits, for example a tenant fit-out or change of occupancy.
- **Operational permits (IFC 2024 §105.5.9).** A permit is needed for "Oxidizing (including oxygen)" compressed gas above **504 cu ft at NTP** [29]. A concentrator stores essentially no gas, so this permit is unlikely to apply (inference). Oxygen cylinders kept on site as backup could trigger it.
- **IFC §5306 (medical gases)** applies to "health care-related facilities intended for patient or veterinary care", and the chapter does not mention concentrators [30].
- **The KFD inspection list** (tanks, LP gas, pyrotechnics, commercial construction, beer, tents, hood suppression, alarms, sprinklers) has **no oxygen or hyperbaric item** [25].
- **Does a mild chamber count as a "hyperbaric facility"?** Knoxville has not addressed this. Evidence that fire officials will say yes:
  - **Minnesota SFM interpretation (undated):** MHBOT chambers at 2 to 4.5 psi with 90 to 95% oxygen by mask "fall under the scope of the MSFC under Section 609" and must meet NFPA 99 Ch. 14, regardless of occupancy. It rejects the argument that Ch. 14 covers only health care [33].
  - **NFPA 99 TIA 1735** (proposal for a "Category 4" for low-pressure chambers, linked in ballot comments to Restore Hyper Wellness) failed **0 to 17** on 2023-07-21 [34]. A committee member wrote that "Existing NFPA 99, Chapter 14 requirements for Category 3 hyperbaric care apply to installations with mild hyperbaric chambers". Another said Ch. 14 applies at 0 to 100 psig and ASME PVHO-1 applies at 2 psi or more.
  - **North Carolina:** Raleigh ordered a stop on 2022-06-25 after an inspector found "no fire suppression system", following Charlotte earlier that month. Durham later issued cease-and-desist letters to two businesses after a state fire-code official found the chambers non-compliant [46][47].
  - **UHMS** says many soft chambers do not meet NFPA 99 Ch. 14 or ASME PVHO-1 [18].
- **What this means for Purebaric:**
  - **Business placements** (gym, studio, chiropractor, salon, team facility): the Knoxville Fire Marshal could treat a chamber plus concentrator as a §609 / NFPA 99 Ch. 14 hyperbaric facility. NFPA 99 Ch. 14 compliance (chamber built to PVHO-1, room housing rules, fire protection, grounding, a trained operator) is generally **not achievable with a soft chamber** (our inference from UHMS and NFPA sources). That makes this a **blocker** for B2B in Knoxville until the fire marshal answers in writing, and in Knox County under the 2018 IFC, which has the same §609.
  - **Homes:** IFC enforcement inside one- and two-family dwellings is limited in practice, and the IRC governs construction there. We found no permit requirement for a home concentrator. Fire-safety duties still apply (NFPA 99 and manufacturer warnings, no smoking or open flame near oxygen). This is a requirement to handle, not a blocker. **Whether Knoxville applies §609 in dwellings is NOT VERIFIED.**
- **Knox County (unincorporated):** the county history document lists **Ord. O-18-10-101 (11/19/18)** adopting the 2018 IFC and 2018 NFPA 101. No later adoption is listed; **the current edition is NOT VERIFIED** [31]. The county Fire Prevention Bureau is at knoxcounty.org/fire.
- **State:** the TN SFMO has adopted the 2021 IFC and IBC (table effective 4/17/2025) [32]. Knoxville enforces its own 2024 adoption.
- **Maryville, Alcoa, Oak Ridge, Farragut:** editions **NOT VERIFIED**. Search results suggest Maryville uses the 2018 IFC and adopted the 2024 IBC. Every IFC edition since 2018 contains §609, so the analysis is the same.

### 7. Zoning and occupancy

- **Knoxville home occupation (Zoning Code §10.3.R)** [35]:
  - no more than three non-resident workers;
  - "clearly incidental and subordinate" to residential use;
  - "Outdoor storage is prohibited";
  - no traffic beyond normal residential levels;
  - "vehicle dispatch for any business where vehicles to be dispatched congregate on site [is] prohibited";
  - "Only products produced on premises may be physically sold from the premises. Internet and mail order sales are permitted";
  - no more than 25% of the dwelling's floor area.
  - **Reading:** storing 5 to 10 boxed chambers **indoors** within 25% of the floor area, with one van and online booking and no customer pickups, looks consistent with these limits. A fleet of vans gathering for dispatch, outdoor storage, or customer pickups at the house would not be. Get a zoning confirmation; whether "rental" counts as "sale" here is **NOT VERIFIED**.
- **Districts for a Knoxville warehouse (§9.2 Use Matrix)** [36]:
  - **Warehouse and Distribution** ("An enclosed facility for the storage and distribution of ... supplies, and/or equipment") is permitted in **I-MU, I-RD, I-G, I-H**.
  - **Office** is permitted in C-N, C-G, C-H, C-R, DK, O, OP, I-MU, I-RD, I-G, I-H and INST.
  - **Enclosed self-storage** is permitted in C-H, C-R, I-MU, I-G and I-H, and special use in C-G.
  - "Heavy Retail, Rental, and Service" (P in C-H, C-R, I-G) is aimed at large outdoor-storage rental and probably does not fit.
- **Host businesses (change of use).** The Knoxville definitions [37] matter:
  - **Personal Service Establishment** includes "beauty shops ... tanning salons ... health clubs".
  - **Medical/Dental Office/Clinic** includes "chiropractors ... alternative medicine clinics ... and medical spas". It is a different use, permitted in C-G and others and special use in C-N, and it is **not** allowed in some districts where personal service is (for example I-G).
  - A gym or salon adding one chamber as an accessory amenity is unlikely to be a zoning change of use. A site that markets "hyperbaric therapy" as a main service risks being classified as a medical spa or clinic. **NOT VERIFIED.** Ask Knoxville-Knox County Planning or the Zoning Administrator.
  - Separately, the **building code** requires a permit for a change of occupancy, and IBC §425 can be triggered at any permit review.
- **Knox County (unincorporated) home occupation (§4.90)** [38]:
  - Code Administration must approve **before** starting (permit form, 865-215-2325);
  - "No person other than household members residing on the premises shall be engaged in such occupation";
  - no more than 25% of the floor area;
  - no outdoor storage "other than vehicles";
  - "No home occupation shall be conducted in any accessory building", so no detached-garage warehouse;
  - "no sales ... other than sales of services or sales of products produced on the premises".
  - Equipment rental from home stock may not fit. **NOT VERIFIED.**

### 8. Health department and cosmetology board

- The Knox County Health Department Environmental Health Division (140 Dameron Ave; 865-215-5200) regulates "restaurants, pools, hotels, daycares, schools, and body arts studios" [39]. No wellness, spa or esthetics-equipment permit was found, so a chamber in a salon or gym falls outside its programs.
- The Tennessee Board of Cosmetology and Barber Examiners (a state TDCI board, not local) inspects shops under Rules ch. 0440-02 [40]. One search found no rule on non-cosmetology services inside licensed salons. Salon owners should ask the Board whether an unrelated wellness device affects their shop licence (state memo to cover).

### 9. Local sales tax

- **TN DOR rate map (read 2026-10-10)** [41]:
  - Knox County **2.25%** (effective 1/1/1989); **Knoxville 2.25%**; **Farragut 2.25%**.
  - Blount County **2.75%** (7/1/2014), covering **Alcoa** and **Maryville**.
  - Anderson County **2.75%** (7/1/2006), including **Oak Ridge** (which also lies partly in Roane County; the Roane portion was not checked).
  - Combined with the 7% state rate: **9.25%** in Knox County, **9.75%** in Blount and Anderson.
- **Single-article cap on rentals.** The TN DOR single-article webinar (Oct 2022) says: "For leases/rentals, the state single article, local article limit, and 7% state rate should be charged on 1st lease payment. Each subsequent lease payment would have just the 7% state tax." Both are charged again on renewal [42].
  - Search results say Letter Ruling 02-07 and SUT-43 instead apply the local cap and the 2.75% tier to **each** payment. **NOT VERIFIED (not read).** This possible conflict is for the state-tax memo or a DOR letter ruling.
  - For a chamber worth over $1,600, the local tax applies only to the first $1,600 of the taxed amount, and the state single-article 2.75% applies from $1,600.01 to $3,200.
