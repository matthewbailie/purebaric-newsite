# Tennessee state-law memo: renting mild hyperbaric chambers with oxygen concentrators (Knoxville expansion)

Prepared 2026-10-10 for Purebaric LLC (Utah). Scope: the Tennessee STATE layer only. Federal FDA rules and
Knoxville / Knox County rules are covered in separate memos.

**This is research, not legal advice.** It was compiled from public sources by a non-lawyer research process. Several
statute texts were read on FindLaw (marked "current as of 2024-01-02") or Cornell LII rather than on the official
Lexis-hosted Tennessee Code, so a Tennessee attorney must confirm current text before anyone relies on it. Items not read
in a primary source are marked **[not verified]**.

---

## (a) Executive summary

1. Tennessee has no hyperbaric-specific statute, rule, facility licence, operator licence, AG opinion or board statement that this research could find. The only 2023-2026 state action naming HBOT is a non-binding 2025 resolution (HJR 1) urging Congress to fund HBOT for veterans [S12].
2. **The biggest issue is the oxygen concentrator in HOME rentals.** Tennessee licenses "home care organizations providing home medical equipment" (HME). The definition expressly includes "oxygen care equipment and oxygen delivery systems" and any device with the federal Rx-only label, **sold or rented** for delivery to a consumer's residence in Tennessee, wherever the provider is based [S4][S5][S6].
3. An HME licence costs $1,404 a year and requires a Health Facilities Commission survey or inspection, written policies, staff-training rules and equipment-maintenance rules. Since 2023 an out-of-state provider can hold one without a Tennessee location [S6][S7][S8].
4. **Respiratory care.** Under a Board of Respiratory Care rule (eff. 2021-06-07), explaining how a concentrator works in someone's residence is exempt from respiratory-therapist licensing only at **under 6 L/min by nasal cannula**. A 10 L/min mask set-up falls in "all other respiratory equipment", where every act except delivery, repair and maintenance counts as the practice of respiratory care [S9][S10]. Purebaric's in-home walkthrough could be read as unlicensed respiratory care.
5. Rentals to businesses (gyms, studios) fall outside the HME definition, which covers only delivery to a residence. But the Pharmacy Act defines "wholesale distribution" as distributing a drug or device "to persons other than the patient or consumer". Whether a B2B rental of Rx devices needs a Board of Pharmacy wholesaler/distributor licence is open [S13][S14][S15].
6. Tennessee's legend-drug law defines "legend drugs" as **"any item"** that federal law limits to prescription dispensing, and makes it unlawful to sell or give one away without a prescription [S16][S17]. Whether that reaches Rx-only devices (concentrator, chamber) is unresolved and should go to counsel.
7. Practice of medicine: anyone who "treats, or professes to diagnose, treat ... any physical ailment" is practising medicine [S1]. Marketing must stay on general-wellness claims. A chamber is not a "cosmetic medical service", so the medical-spa registry should not apply unless the chamber is sold as an appearance treatment [S2][S3].
8. Waivers: Tennessee enforces adult releases case by case under *Copeland* (Tenn. 2018). A release cannot cover gross negligence, recklessness or intentional wrongdoing, and **a parent cannot waive a child's claims** (*Childress*, *Blackwell*) [S19]-[S23].
9. Taxes: rentals are taxable sales (7% state plus about 2.25% Knox local) [S26][S27][S28]. In-state delivery and set-up create physical nexus. A foreign-LLC certificate of authority is required ($50 per member, $300 minimum) [S31]. Franchise and excise tax applies to LLCs [S32]. Business-tax thresholds are $3,000 and $100,000 per jurisdiction [S29][S30].
10. Fire: the state adopted the 2021 IFC and IBC effective 2025-04-17. Neither NFPA 99 nor hyperbaric chambers appear in the state's adopted-codes list [S33]. No state position on soft chambers was found.

## (c) Detailed findings

### Topic 1. Hyperbaric licensing and the practice of medicine

- **No hyperbaric facility type.** Tenn. Code Ann. § 68-11-201 lists every facility type the Health Facilities Commission licenses: hospital, ASTC, outpatient diagnostic center, home care organization, renal dialysis clinic, recuperation center, rehabilitation facility and others. None mentions hyperbaric, oxygen therapy, wellness or spa [S4]. Searches of Tennessee rules and Department of Health materials found no rule naming hyperbaric chambers [S11]. (Absence of evidence; a rules-database word search by counsel is advisable.)
- **Medical Practice Act.** § 63-6-204(a)(1) says a person practises medicine if they "treat[] or profess[] to diagnose, treat, operate on or prescribe for any physical ailment or any physical injury to or deformity of another". It also covers anyone who, "using clinical judgment, determines whether a treatment or procedure is clinically appropriate" [S1]. Renting a chamber with no diagnosis, no treatment selection and no ailment claims fits poorly within the definition. Advertising that a session treats injuries, concussion, long-COVID, wounds and so on, or staff advising which customers "should" use it for a condition, moves toward "professes to ... treat". Unlicensed practice is a criminal offence (Class B misdemeanor per a secondary summary **[not verified]**).
- **Analogous wellness guidance.** Tennessee requires any entity offering "cosmetic medical services" to register as a medical spa with a supervising Tennessee physician (§ 63-1-153; Rule 0880-02-.24) [S2][S3]. The definition covers devices or energy that alter living tissue "to improve the patient's appearance", so a chamber marketed for recovery is outside it. A chamber marketed in a salon as a skin or anti-aging treatment could draw the question. No Tennessee board statement or AG opinion on IV hydration, cryotherapy or hyperbaric by non-licensed operators was found. The only mention seen was an Osteopathic Board discussion (minutes, 2025-05-07) noting national FSMB work on IV hydration [S18]. By contrast, a 1996 Texas AG opinion treated HBOT as medical practice the Texas board may regulate (other state, persuasive only).

### Topic 2. Medical oxygen, concentrators, HME/DME and pharmacy licences

**HME licence (Health Facilities Commission).**
- § 68-11-201(26)(A) defines "home medical equipment" as equipment "intended for use by the consumer". It includes (i) any device whose label says "Caution: Federal law requires dispensing by or on the order of a physician" and (v) "oxygen care equipment and oxygen delivery systems" [S4].
- § 68-11-201(28) defines "home medical equipment services" as service by anyone "who sells or rents home medical equipment for delivery to the consumer's place of residence in this state, regardless of the location of the home medical equipment provider" [S4].
- Rule 0720-30-.01 repeats these definitions [S5]. Rule 0720-30-.02 says no person may operate "any home care organization providing home medical equipment" without a licence. The annual fee is $1,404, an inspection or survey is required, and the rule was amended effective 2024-06-02 [S6].
- Rule 0720-30-.06 adds operating duties [S7]: qualified delivery and set-up staff, written patient-education guidelines, maintenance to the manufacturer's guidelines, FDA-recall tracking, and separation of clean and dirty equipment. The definition of "medical record" (0720-30-.01) assumes a physician order exists ("the medical record will consist of the physician order only"), but no rule found makes an order a licensing condition [S5][S7].
- A 2023 change ("Quinniee's Law", Pub. Ch. 99 of 2023) removed the in-state location requirement [S8].
- **Effect:** A 10 L concentrator is an "oxygen delivery system". Renting one for delivery to a Tennessee home appears to be HME services needing a licence, whether rented or sold and whether or not a prescription exists. A soft chamber that carries the federal Rx legend is arguably HME too. The statute quotes the old "Caution: Federal law ..." wording, while modern labels say "Rx only", so the fit needs counsel. Renting changes nothing: the definition says "sells or rents".
- **Business placements** (gym, chiropractor, studio) are not "delivery to the consumer's place of residence", so the HME licence should not apply to them.

**Respiratory care licensing.**
- Rule 1330-01-.03 (eff. 2021-06-07) covers respiratory equipment "used by a patient in the patient's place of residence" [S9][S10]:
  - Attaching equipment to the patient and instructing the patient count as respiratory care (para. 1).
  - Explaining concentrators is exempt only for "less than 6.00 liters per minute ... through nasal cannula only" (para. 2(c)).
  - For "all other respiratory equipment", every act "except delivery, repair and maintenance" is respiratory care (para. 3).
- The statutory definition (§ 63-27-102) frames the practice as care of patients with cardiorespiratory deficiencies under physician supervision, including "administration of ... medical gasses" [S10]. A wellness user is arguably not a "patient", but the rule's text fits Purebaric's 10 L/min mask walkthrough uncomfortably well. Possible fixes: a licensed RT does home set-ups, the walkthrough is limited to delivery and assembly with manufacturer materials, or flow and interface are changed. Counsel should advise.

**Board of Pharmacy.**
- Rule 1140-09-.01(1) says every "manufacturer, outsourcing facility, oxygen supplier or wholesaler/distributor, before engaging in the manufacture, sale or distribution of prescription drugs and prescription devices in this state, must be licensed" [S13]. Rule 1140-01-.08 requires the licence and an inspection before opening, including for out-of-state entities [S14].
- "Oxygen supplier" (Rule 1140-01-.01) means one who sells or distributes **medical gases** that require a prescription and are federal legend drugs, per the search-tool summary of the rule text [S15]. A concentrator produces oxygen on site and is not a sale of medical gas, so the oxygen-supplier licence seems a poor fit. **[Board position on concentrators not found.]**
- Pharmacy Act § 63-10-204 defines "Wholesale distribution" as distributing a drug or device "to persons other than the patient or consumer", and "Distribute" to include "sale, purchase, trade, delivery, handling, storage, or receipt" [S16]. Renting Rx devices to gyms (not end users) could be read as wholesale distribution. This is an open question for counsel or the Board.
- Medical oxygen as a drug: § 53-10-101 treats any federally Rx-only item as a legend drug, so cylinder or liquid medical oxygen is a legend drug in Tennessee [S17]. Purebaric does not supply cylinders.

### Topic 3. Furnishing Rx-labelled devices without a prescription

- § 53-10-101(a) defines "legend drugs" as **any item** that federal law prohibits dispensing without a prescription from a licensed doctor, dentist, optometrist or veterinarian (blood excluded) [S17]. § 53-10-104(a) makes it unlawful to possess, sell, barter or give away a legend drug except on a qualifying written prescription and as dispensed by a pharmacist [S17b]. § 53-10-105 limits lawful possession and lists exemptions for manufacturers, wholesalers and practitioners [S17c]. The Part is titled "legend drugs" and uses drug language throughout ("compounded or dispensed by a ... pharmacist"), so a court may read "item" as limited to drugs. No Tennessee case or AG opinion applying it to devices was found. The penalty section was not read **[not verified]**.
- Tennessee FD&C Act § 53-1-109: a drug or device is misbranded if its labeling is "false or misleading in any particular" or lacks "adequate directions for use" [S24]. It does not itself make dispensing an Rx device without a prescription an offence. False wellness labeling or relabeling could still be misbranding.
- **Bottom line:** No Tennessee provision was found that plainly bans renting an Rx-only device to a consumer without a prescription. Two provisions create real ambiguity: § 53-10-104's "any item", and the HME rules' assumption that a physician order exists. Options: buy non-Rx (OTC or general-wellness) equipment where it exists, add a physician-order pathway, or get a written opinion.

### Topic 4. Tennessee Consumer Protection Act and health claims

- Unlawful acts include representing that goods have "sponsorship, approval, characteristics, ingredients, uses, benefits" they lack (§ 47-18-104(b)(5)) and misrepresenting "standard, quality or grade" ((b)(7)). The catch-all, "any other act or practice which is deceptive" ((b)(27)), is enforceable only by the Attorney General [S25].
- Private suits (§ 47-18-109) need an "ascertainable loss" and may not be class actions (secondary summary) [S25b]. The TCPA has no special health-device rule. Deceptive wellness claims would be judged under (b)(5)/(b)(7), with FTC-style substantiation as the practical benchmark.
- No Tennessee AG action against hyperbaric, stem-cell or wellness-device marketers was found. The AG has used the TCPA against a fertility practice (2024) [S34].
- The legislature's 2026 stem-cell act (HB 2246, Pub. Ch. 1016, signed 2026-05-26) lets physicians offer non-FDA-approved regenerative therapies, with advertising, informed-consent and adverse-event rules [S35]. That signals a "permit with disclosure" posture toward unapproved wellness therapies, limited to physicians.
- Auto-renewal: 2024 SB 1894 (eff. 2024-07-01) added a notice duty when an automatic renewal charge falls more than 60 days after consent (secondary sources; cited as § 47-18-133 **[not verified]**) [S36]. This matters if 1-12 month terms auto-renew.

### Topic 5. Waivers, releases and minors

- *Copeland v. HealthSouth/Methodist Rehabilitation Hospital, LP*, 565 S.W.3d 260 (Tenn. 2018-12-20) set the test: enforceability depends on the totality of circumstances, weighing (1) relative bargaining power, (2) clarity of the exculpatory language and (3) public policy and public interest [S19][S20]. The release in that case (medical transport, signed by a patient) was held unenforceable.
- *Copeland*, as quoted in *Milan Supply Chain Solutions v. Navistar* (Tenn. 2021-08-02), also says a party may not exempt itself from "liability for gross negligence, reckless conduct, or intentional wrongdoing" [S21].
- The "medical" angle cuts against Purebaric. *Copeland* weighed the public interest of health-related services. The more Purebaric looks like a health-care provider, the weaker its waiver.
- Post-*Copeland*, the Court of Appeals enforced a gym membership waiver in *Torres v. YMCA of Middle Tennessee*, finding it clear and prominent and rejecting late gross-negligence allegations (reported 2026-05-14; docket and date of decision **[not verified]**) [S22]. *Boswell v. YMCA* (2019) did not reach the waiver.
- **Minors:** *Childress v. Madison County*, 777 S.W.2d 1 (Tenn. Ct. App. 1989) and *Blackwell v. Sky High Sports Nashville Operations, LLC*, 523 S.W.3d 624 (Tenn. Ct. App. 2017-01-09; perm. app. denied 2017) hold that a parent cannot sign away a minor's pre-injury claims, including through indemnity clauses. *Blackwell* also refused a foreign choice-of-law clause with no material connection to the deal [S23]. So a Utah choice-of-law clause in Purebaric's waiver may not be honored for Tennessee customers.
- Relevance: home rentals where children use or play near the chamber, and any school or youth-team placements. The waiver will not protect against the child's own claim. Insurance, written child-use rules ("no unsupervised minors", "no use by minors without physician clearance") and the business customer's own supervision duties carry the risk.

### Topic 6. Taxes and registration (confirmation)

- **Sales tax on rentals:** § 67-6-204 taxes leases and rentals of tangible personal property at the retail rate. Revenue's leasing notice states the tax applies to "all leases and rentals of tangible personal property" [S26][S27]. One exception: if the owner keeps continuous supervision and supplies an operator, the transaction is a non-taxable service (§ 67-6-204(b)). That does not fit unattended rentals. Revenue letter ruling 18-04 says remote monitoring alone does not count as supplying an operator [S27b]. Rate: 7% state plus 2.25% Knox County, 9.25% combined (secondary sources; confirm with Revenue's rate lookup) [S28]. Single-article and local-cap treatment of periodic lease payments was not researched **[open]**.
- **Medical exemption § 67-6-314:**
  - (2) exempts "durable medical equipment for home use sold pursuant to a prescription", so it needs a prescription [S37].
  - (3) covers oxygen-delivery repair parts, single-patient components and disposable supplies.
  - Revenue letter ruling 08-15 (2008-02-27) concluded that oxygen concentrators and masks were exempt under (3) with "no prescription ... required", and that rentals qualify like sales because "sale" includes a lease [S38].
  - That ruling binds only its addressee. The current text of (3) as read on FindLaw lists parts, components and supplies, not the equipment itself. Do not rely on any exemption without a new letter ruling; the chamber itself would not qualify.
- **Business tax:** Under the Tennessee Works Tax Act (Pub. Ch. 377 of 2023, Notice 23-08), businesses with under $100,000 gross receipts per jurisdiction file no return [S29][S30]. Those with $3,000 to $100,000 need a $15 minimal-activity licence from the county and/or city. At $100,000 or more a standard licence and annual return are required. Classification for equipment rental was not confirmed **[not verified]**.
- **Foreign LLC:** A certificate of authority is required (Form SS-4233; cited §§ 48-249-904/-905 per secondary sources) [S31]. The fee is $50 per member, minimum $300, maximum $3,000. Operating before registering carries a penalty of three times the fee for each year **[secondary]**.
- **Nexus:** Delivering, setting up and keeping company-owned chambers at Tennessee addresses is physical presence. Nexus for sales tax, business tax and franchise and excise tax follows regardless of economic-nexus thresholds (inference; economic-nexus thresholds were not researched).
- **Franchise and excise tax:** LLCs are subject. The 2024 law (Pub. Ch. 950) repealed the property measure, so franchise tax is now 0.25% of apportioned net worth [S32]. The 6.5% excise rate and $100 minimums are **[not verified]** in this research. This is a real cost difference from Utah, where an LLC is generally a pass-through.

### Topic 7. 2023-2026 legislation and state agency posture

- **HJR 1 (114th G.A.), sponsored by Rep. Reedy, signed by the Governor 2025-05-05.** It "strongly urge[s]" Congress to enact legislation giving veterans access to TBI/PTSD treatments "that include hyperbaric oxygen therapy" [S12]. It is non-binding but shows a favourable state posture.
- No 2023-2026 Tennessee bill on HBOT insurance mandates, facility rules or a state veteran HBOT programme was found (search-based; not exhaustive).
- No Health Facilities Commission or Department of Health rule naming hyperbaric chambers was found (see Topic 1).

### Topic 8. State fire and life safety

- The State Fire Marshal's Office currently adopts the 2021 IBC, 2021 IFC, 2018 IRC, 2017 NEC and others, effective 2025-04-17 [S33]. NFPA 101 (2021) appears only for certain small board-and-care conversions and classroom-locking provisions; the 2025 update stopped using NFPA 101 for state buildings and schools. NFPA 99 and hyperbaric chambers are not mentioned.
- NFPA 99's hyperbaric chapter therefore has no state-level hook for gyms, salons or homes. It applies through licensed health-care facility rules and CMS (**[not verified]** for Tennessee HFC rules).
- No State Fire Marshal statement on oxygen-enriched soft chambers was found. IFC oxygen provisions (compressed-gas storage) are aimed at cylinders, not concentrators. Local fire officials may still apply general IFC hazard provisions; see the Knoxville memo.

### Topic 9. Utah comparison (only verifiable differences)

- **HME licence.** Tennessee has an explicit Health Facilities Commission licence for anyone who sells or rents oxygen delivery systems or Rx-legend devices for delivery to Tennessee homes [S4][S6]. This research found no Utah equivalent facility licence. Utah instead regulates "durable medical equipment" and "medical gas provider" facilities as Class E pharmacies under its Pharmacy Practice Act rules (R156-17b-617d, -617f) [S39][S40]. Whether Purebaric's Utah model needs a Utah Class E licence was not researched and is **[not verified]**. The Tennessee requirement is clearer and plainly reaches rentals.
- **Respiratory care.** Tennessee's flow-rate and cannula-based exemption (Rule 1330-01-.03) is unusually specific [S9]. Utah's respiratory-care act was not compared **[not verified]**.
- **Waivers and minors.** Both states refuse to let a parent release a child's claims in general: Tennessee in *Childress* and *Blackwell*, Utah in *Hawkins v. Peart*, 2001 UT 94 [S41]. Utah's holding is from the case listing plus background knowledge, **[holding not verified from opinion text]**. No material difference.
- **Entity taxes.** Tennessee imposes franchise and excise tax on LLCs and a gross-receipts business tax [S29][S32]. Utah has neither a gross-receipts business tax nor an entity-level tax on a pass-through LLC **[Utah side not verified in this research]**. Both states tax equipment rentals as sales.

## (d) Open questions for a Tennessee attorney

1. Does renting a 10 L/min oxygen concentrator, and/or an Rx-labelled soft chamber, for delivery to a Tennessee residence require an HME licence under § 68-11-201(26)-(28) and Rule 0720-30-.02 when there is no prescription and the use is "wellness"? Does the old "Caution: Federal law ..." wording in (26)(A)(i) reach "Rx only" labels?
2. If licensed, how do the HME rules (patient records built on a physician order, staff qualifications) work for a no-prescription wellness model? Would the Commission license it at all?
3. Is Purebaric's in-home walkthrough (mask, 10 L/min) the practice of respiratory care under Rule 1330-01-.03(3), given that § 63-27-102 is framed around patients and physician supervision? What exact script keeps it within "delivery, repair and maintenance"?
4. Does § 53-10-101's "any item" bring Rx-only devices within the legend-drug sale ban of § 53-10-104? What is the penalty?
5. Does renting Rx devices to gyms and clinics (not end users) count as "wholesale distribution" (§ 63-10-204) requiring a Board of Pharmacy wholesaler/distributor licence (Rule 1140-09-.01)? Would the Board treat a concentrator lessor as an "oxygen supplier"?
6. Would sourcing non-Rx (OTC or general-wellness) concentrators and chambers, or adding a telehealth physician-order step, remove issues 1-5? Which creates less exposure?
7. Waivers: will a Utah choice-of-law or forum clause be honoured? What wording maximises *Copeland* clarity? Can parents give an enforceable indemnity for their own claims (not the child's)? How should B2B contracts allocate end-user claims (indemnity, additional-insured status)?
8. Tax: are the concentrator or mask components of a bundled rental exempt under § 67-6-314(3) after the 2008 ruling (seek a letter ruling)? How does the single-article cap apply to monthly rental payments? What is the business-tax classification for equipment rental?
9. Does marketing for "recovery", "inflammation" or "sleep" in Tennessee risk "professes to ... treat" under § 63-6-204? Would a salon placement marketed for skin trigger the medical-spa registration (§ 63-1-153)?
