# Critique: SALES-REP-TRAINING.md, version 3

Reviewed 10 October 2026 by two readers: a field sales manager who has trained gym and spa reps, and the owner. Facts checked against business.html, index.html, how-it-works.html, faq.html and pricing.html; the live rates at purebaric.com/api/pricing; the Utah business agreement; the Tennessee business agreement; the seven Tennessee pamphlets; and knoxville/hbot_providers.md.

## Verdict

**Needs work before it goes out.**

This version reads like a person talking. The rate table matches the live prices to the dollar, the money math is right, and the claims rules are short. What stops it shipping: a rep who follows it will push 12-month terms without telling the owner what leaving early costs, will understate the insurance the owner has to carry, will say "Yes" to "Is it safe?", and will tell an owner "four a day is the ceiling" while holding a pamphlet that says six. A handful of spoken lines still use words nobody says in a doorway.

## Fix these

### 1. The early-exit cost is missing

- **Where:** Section 2, "How it ends." Quote: "After the term they chose, it continues month to month. Either side can end it with 30 days' notice and we pick the chamber up at no charge." Section 6 then offers "twelve at $783 if they are confident."
- **What's wrong:** The Tennessee agreement, Section 2.1, re-bills the months already used at the published rate for the term actually completed if the business leaves early. The document never says this. A rep reading section 2 will tell an owner they can walk away on 30 days' notice at the 12-month rate.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** An owner signs for 12 months at $783, leaves after four, and gets a surprise bill of $392. That is a refund fight and a bad review in a small town.
- **Fix:** Replace the paragraph with:
  > **How it ends.** They pick a term and the rate is locked for it. After the term it runs month to month. Either side can end it with 30 days' notice by email, and we pick the chamber up free. If they leave before the term is up, we re-bill the months they used at the rate for the length they actually stayed. Example: sign for 12 months at $783, leave after 4, and those 4 months are re-billed at $881, so they owe $392. Tell them this before they pick a term.

### 2. The "Is it safe?" answer opens with a promise and gets a fact wrong

- **Where:** Section 7. Quote: "Yes, you've probably seen the one in Michigan. That was a hard chamber filled with pure oxygen at a clinic. Ours is a soft chamber at low pressure, and in Tennessee we're starting without any oxygen enrichment at all."
- **What's wrong:** Four things. "Yes" is a flat safety promise, and the agreement itself (Section 7.1) lists risks that "cannot be eliminated". The Troy, Michigan chamber ran high-concentration oxygen; "pure" overstates what we know. "At a clinic" softens the point that matters most: it was a wellness clinic, the same kind of business we sell to. "The one" implies a single incident; there was also a 2025 Arizona wellness-clinic death. And the answer has no Utah version. A Utah rep who says "without any oxygen enrichment" is saying something false, because Utah chambers come with a concentrator and a mask.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The owner who read the news hears a rep shade the facts on the one question where trust decides the deal. In Utah, the rep makes a false statement.
- **Fix:**
  > **"Is it safe? I've read about chamber fires."** "Fair question. The fire you read about in Michigan was a hard chamber running high-concentration oxygen at a wellness clinic. Ours is a soft chamber at lower pressure, and here in Tennessee it runs on regular air, no added oxygen. The rules are simple: nothing that burns near it, no oil-based lotions, and your staff starts and ends every session."
  >
  > **In Utah,** swap the middle sentence for: "Ours is a soft chamber at lower pressure. The chamber is filled with air, and the client breathes oxygen through a mask, which is why the no-flame, no-oil rules matter."
  >
  > If they bring up a different incident, don't guess. Say Michael will follow up.

### 3. The insurance line understates what the owner must carry

- **Where:** Section 2, "Insurance." Quote: "They keep their normal business liability policy and ask their broker to add us as additional insured. Most brokers do it in a day."
- **What's wrong:** Both agreements (Section 5.3) require liability coverage of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence and $2,000,000 aggregate that covers the sessions, and a certificate before the first session. A "normal" gym or salon policy may not cover chamber sessions at all. "Most brokers do it in a day" has no source.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The owner says yes, calls the broker, learns the policy needs an endorsement or a higher limit, and the install stalls. Or worse, the chamber goes in under a policy that does not cover it.
- **Fix:**
  > **Insurance.** They need business liability insurance of at least $1 million per incident and $2 million total that covers the chamber sessions, with Purebaric added as additional insured. They send us the certificate before the first session. Tell them to call their broker this week. Some policies need a change before they cover chamber sessions.

### 4. The liability answer gets the equipment split wrong and talks like a lawyer

- **Where:** Section 7. Quote: "The agreement puts the equipment on us and the running of sessions on you, and it does ask you to release and indemnify us broadly, so have your attorney read it. Most do."
- **What's wrong:** "The equipment on us" is half true. We maintain and repair for normal wear (Section 6.1), but the business pays for damage, loss and theft at its location (Section 6.3). "Release and indemnify us broadly" is legal vocabulary a rep will mumble and an owner will not understand. The sentence runs 31 words.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The owner believes a broken or stolen chamber is our problem, then gets an itemized damage bill. The rep sounds like he is reading a contract.
- **Fix:**
  > **"What about liability?"** "Your clients sign a consent form before their first session. You carry liability insurance that covers the sessions and add us to it. We maintain and repair the chamber. You run the sessions, and damage or theft at your place is on you. If a client sues over a session, the agreement has you cover it, including our legal costs. Have your attorney read it before you sign."

### 5. The session ceiling contradicts the pamphlet, and section 3 breaks its own rule

- **Where:** Section 3. Quotes: "Use the pamphlet's numbers and nothing else." / "four a day is the ceiling for a popular room." / "So $60 to $90 is well under the market and still clears the rental in a few sessions a week." / "Hospital sessions are several hundred dollars."
- **What's wrong:** All seven Tennessee pamphlets show "Strong: 6 a day, 156 sessions" and say "One chamber can run up to 8 sessions in a 10-hour day." The rep's table uses 26, 52 and 104 sessions; the pamphlet uses 52, 104 and 156. The "$60 to $90" range and the hospital comparison appear in no pamphlet, which breaks the rule two lines earlier. The hospital line also invites a medical comparison we tell reps to avoid.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The owner reads six a day on the pamphlet and hears the rep call four the ceiling. Either the rep looks like he does not know his own material, or the pamphlet looks inflated.
- **Fix:** Change the rule to "Use the numbers below and the pamphlet's, nothing else." Keep the 26/52/104 table; the math is right. Replace "One or two sessions a day... popular room" with:
  > The pamphlet shows two, four and six a day. Start the conversation lower: one or two a day is a realistic first few months. A chamber can run eight in a ten-hour day, so four a day is a busy room, not a full one.

  Delete the "$60 to $90" sentence and the hospital sentence.

### 6. The doorway pitch quotes one price and section 6 offers another

- **Where:** Section 5, step 3. Quote: "The 12-month rate is $783 a month; at $75 a session that's paid back in about eleven sessions." Section 6 offers "three months at $929" to five of the seven business types. "Too expensive" in section 7 repeats "paid for by about eleven sessions a month."
- **What's wrong:** The rep leads with $783 and eleven sessions, then offers $929, where break-even is 13. That feels like a bait and switch. "Paid back" sounds like a loan.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The owner remembers $783 and balks at $929.
- **Fix:** Step 3:
  > "We bring the chamber, set it up, train your staff and look after it. You charge what you want for an hour and keep it. It's $929 a month on three months or $783 on a year, plus tax. Eleven to thirteen sessions a month covers it."

  "Too expensive":
  > "On a year it's $783 a month, and eleven sessions at $75 covers it. On three months it's thirteen. So the question is whether you can sell three a week to people who already pay you for recovery. If you can't, this isn't for you, and I'd rather hear that now."

### 7. The tax line conflicts with the pamphlet

- **Where:** Section 2. Quote: "Sales tax, if any applies, is calculated by Stripe at checkout, so never quote a tax rate or add tax to a number."
- **What's wrong:** Every Tennessee pamphlet says "plus sales tax", "Prices exclude Tennessee sales tax" and "Sales tax applies to the rental." The document says "if any applies." The sentence is also passive and 25 words.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The rep tells an owner tax may not apply; the first invoice shows tax; the owner feels misled.
- **Fix:**
  > Prices are plus Tennessee sales tax. Stripe adds it at checkout. Say "plus tax" and never quote a rate.

### 9. Section 1 has a wrong number, skips what the staff does, and lets the legal memo back in

- **Where:** Section 1. Quotes: "roughly what you feel ten to fifteen feet underwater" / "while our attorney and the local fire officials confirm what they want from us" / "That one sentence keeps us out of trouble and makes the business's life easier too, because they do not have to be a clinic to offer it."
- **What's wrong:** 1.5 ATA is about 16 to 17 feet of seawater, not ten to fifteen; the website does not use the underwater comparison at all. The attorney and fire officials clause is the material the owner rejected last round. "Keeps us out of trouble" is a lecture. And the section skips three things an owner wants in the first minute, all of which are in the website or pamphlet: what the staff member does, how long it takes them (about 15 minutes a session, per the pamphlet), and how many sessions a chamber can run (up to 8 in a 10-hour day, one person at a time). Cleaning between sessions (business.html: "Your staff wipes the chamber down") is missing too.
- **What happens if you don't fix it:** The first question every owner asks is "Who runs it and how long does it take my people?" The rep has no answer in the document.
- **Fix:** Replace the first paragraph and add a second:
  > We rent mild hyperbaric chambers. It's a soft-shell pod a person lies down in. A quiet compressor beside it slowly raises the air pressure inside to about one and a half times normal. Ears may pop, like on a plane. The person relaxes, naps, reads or uses their phone for about an hour. Then the pressure comes down over a few minutes and they step out.
  >
  > The staff member checks the client signed the consent form, helps them in, zips the chamber closed and starts the pressure. They stay in the building during the session, then let the pressure down, help the client out and wipe the chamber down for the next person. That's about 15 minutes of staff time a session. One person at a time; a chamber can run up to eight sessions in a ten-hour day.

  Replace the Tennessee paragraph with:
  > In Utah the chambers also come with an oxygen concentrator and a mask. In Tennessee we start without it. If a business asks about oxygen, say: "Here we're starting with regular air. Michael can tell you if and when we add oxygen."

  Replace "What we are not" with:
  > **What we are not.** We're not a clinic and we don't treat anything. Describe it the way you'd describe a sauna or a cold plunge: something people book to relax. That's also why the business doesn't need to be a clinic to offer it.

## Your call

- **The "Angle:" label** appears six times in section 6. It reads like a consultant's deck, but it works as a cheat-sheet label. Rename it "Why it fits" if it bothers you.
- **"Don't say 'not a medical device'."** Section 8 forbids it, but the fine print on every Tennessee pamphlet says "As offered here it is not a medical device." Either add one line ("If they point at the pamphlet's fine print, let it stand and move on") or bring the pamphlet into line.
- **Masks in an air-only chamber.** The pamphlets, the Tennessee agreement (50 single-use masks) and the $1.30 supplies figure all assume a mask. Without oxygen, Tennessee clients do not need one. Keep $1.30 as a cleaning-and-supplies figure or lower it; either way, match the pamphlet.
- **High schools.** Adults-only until the insurer confirms makes nearly every high-school athlete ineligible. Consider dropping high schools from the day-one list and keeping colleges.
- **"Costs less to run" than cryo** (section 6, recovery studios). No source compares running costs. Cut it or keep it as obvious puffery.
- **Install photos.** Utah install photos show the oxygen concentrator. Pick ones that don't, or explain the difference.
- **Fire-official step in section 9.** "Michael also gets the fire official's written okay for that address before we schedule" is process, not pitch, and it explains why reps never promise an install date. Keep it there, out of the spoken lines.
- **Utah term pricing.** The Utah agreement's Exhibit A prints $979 a month and has no initial-term clause, so a Utah rep offering $832 for six months has no contract term behind it. Outside this document's Tennessee focus, but worth fixing in the Utah agreement.
